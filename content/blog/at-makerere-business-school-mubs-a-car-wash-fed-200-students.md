I stood at the main gate of MUBS and watched students stop cars and sell coupons. A coupon started at twenty thousand shillings, though drivers could give more. Most of the students collecting the money were in Year One, but the team was a mix of years.

The campaign is called **Njala Campaign: No Student Should Sleep Hungry**. It is led by Twesigye Denison, a Year Two Bachelor of Commerce student and Minister of Internal Affairs and Welfare. The students had spent the whole week telling people about it before the car wash began.

![Twesigye Denison leads the Njala team as they set out to deliver food packs to students in hostels, 14 November 2026.](https://res.cloudinary.com/jxgiku8t/image/upload/v1794770197/IMG_2645.jpg "Twesigye Denison leads the Njala campaign team on the walk to the hostels, 14 November 2026. The car wash funds became food packs for about two hundred students.")

The coupons were printed in a book, each with the campaign name, the donor’s name and their car’s number plate. Students went from office to office, and when someone paid, they washed that car. “It depends how much you’re willing to give,” Denison told me.      

The money was never meant to be handed out as cash. Students in need would register through a link shared in class groups, with their name and where they stay. Then the team would buy food and pack it.

On 13 November, they washed more than fifty cars and raised over one million shillings. The next day, they spent it on spaghetti, rice, sugar, cooking oil and pads, and packed everything into polythene bags. I followed them as they carried the bags to the Small Gate area and then to Kataza, where most MUBS students live. About two hundred students received a pack.

## **“People keep quiet and carry that burden”**

![Portrait of Lamony Reagan Moses, a Year Two student at Makerere University Business School, he is wearing a light gray t-shirt with tents in the background and a car on his right ](https://res.cloudinary.com/jxgiku8t/image/upload/v1794773394/IMG_2263.jpg "Lamony Reagan Moses, a Year Two student from Gulu, who helped wash cars in the Njala campaign.")

Lamony Reagan Moses, a Year Two student from Gulu, was one of the students washing cars. He told me about a colleague who spent two days on nothing but cassava bought for five hundred shillings, and water.

“That’s a sign to feel that people are really scared,” he said. “People tend to keep quiet and carry that burden of hunger by themselves. They don’t tell others that it’s there.”

Many students come from struggling backgrounds, he explained, and are given a small upkeep with the instruction to budget hard. “At campus, it’s not just only feeding which is your big problem. There’s printing work… you might get malaria… internet.” After those costs, the feeding money is almost gone. Some fear to call home because things are also hard there, so they rely on friends or small survival gigs.

## **“I had no energy”**

![A young man in a dark shirt holds papers and speaks to a circle of students standing on a red dirt field, with tall palm trees and a white tent behind them.](https://res.cloudinary.com/jxgiku8t/image/upload/v1794770246/IMG_2256.jpg "On 13 November 2026, Larry Gift Okullo briefs students before the Njala campaign car wash, splitting them into five groups: some to fetch water, others to wash the cars.")

Larry Gift Okullo, a Year Two Bachelor of Commerce student, described the shock of coming from boarding school, where meals were simply provided.

“When I came to campus, I actually didn’t come with anything. I didn’t even buy sugar, nothing… I just go with money. Whatever I want to buy, I just buy.”

He found himself spending about fifteen thousand shillings a day on breakfast, lunch and supper. One time the money was delayed, and he went roughly two and a half days with almost nothing.

“I had no energy… I could not stand. By the third day I had a headache, I was feeling dizzy.” He was watching a Uganda match but could not follow it. His roommate later helped him.

Then there is the constant trade-off. “Should I buy data or I eat food? So we get caught up in that way.”

## **The numbers behind it**

Students told me hostels near campus start from about Shs 900,000 *per semester*. Data costs about Shs 3,000 a gigabyte, because there is no free Wi-Fi at campus. A day of food money buys five gigabytes. Government-sponsored students receive an allowance of around seven hundred thousand shillings a semester, and many had not received it this semester because of delays. Parents often assume that is enough. Students say it is not.

## **Not waiting for a bigger system**

Denison sees this as a beginning. “This is the start of this campaign,” he said. “By next time, you never know. This campaign can even increase… It might not be a car wash. It can be a run.”

The students handing out bags on 14 November were not waiting for anyone to fix this for them. They washed the cars, counted the money, packed the food and walked it to their colleagues’ doors.

The hunger is real. So is the decision not to let each other carry it alone.
